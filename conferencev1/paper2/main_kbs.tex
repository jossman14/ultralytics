\begin{abstract}
Online gambling promotion spreads rapidly across social media through brand
logos and short videos, often reaching young audiences and, in many countries,
breaking the law. Manual review cannot keep up, so automated visual detection is
needed; a deployable detector must be both accurate on small logos and fast
enough for high-throughput moderation. We study this problem on two
complementary Indonesian datasets: a five-class gambling-logo detection set
(443 images) and a two-class Instagram-reel screening set (3000 whole frames).
We first run a large controlled design-space search of 786 five-fold training
runs over 78 YOLOv8-family variants that recombine downsampling, backbone, and
attention blocks. The search yields two findings that matter for practice. Most
added complexity hurts: only 16.5\% of the variants beat the plain baseline on
the hard dataset, and heavy multi-scale fusion can collapse catastrophically on
the easy one. Architecture ranking also transfers poorly between the two
datasets (Spearman $\rho=0.49$), so single-benchmark model selection is unsafe.
Guided by the search, we identify an efficient winning recipe --- space-to-depth
downsampling, ShuffleNet blocks, and parameter-free SimAM attention --- and
propose a detector that reaches the best accuracy on the hard dataset
(mAP50 $0.884$, recall $0.833$) among all standard and modified nano detectors,
including the newer YOLO11n, YOLO12n, and YOLO26n, while running at $115$ FPS on
a mid-range GPU. Improvements over the baseline are consistent across folds but
not statistically significant at five folds, which we report openly. Grad-CAM
and confusion-matrix analyses show the detector attends to brand marks and
confirm that the residual error concentrates on one small, text-like logo class.
We release the datasets split protocol, the full search results, latency
measurements, and reproducible code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Online gambling promotion has grown into a large content-moderation problem.
Operators advertise betting brands with logos and short videos on social
platforms. This content often targets young and vulnerable audiences. In many
jurisdictions, including Indonesia, the activity is illegal. Platforms and
regulators must find and remove such content at scale. Human review does not
scale to the daily volume of uploads. Automated visual detection is therefore an
attractive first line of defence.

The task is harder than it first appears. A gambling logo may be a small overlay
on a busy video frame. It sits among sports scenes, faces, and other graphics. A
useful detector must localise these small marks reliably. It must also run fast
enough for high-throughput screening. Accuracy alone is not enough. A model that
is accurate but slow cannot be deployed on a moderation pipeline that processes
millions of frames.

A common response is to add architectural complexity to a detector. Practitioners
graft attention modules, multi-scale blocks, and heavier backbones onto a base
model. On large benchmarks these additions often help. On the small,
domain-specific datasets that characterise real moderation tasks, the benefit is
far less certain. Extra capacity can overfit and can slow inference for no gain.
Which modifications actually help, and under what conditions, is rarely studied
systematically for this domain.

This paper takes a systematic, deployment-oriented view of gambling-promotion
detection. We assemble two complementary Indonesian datasets. The first is a
fine-grained gambling-logo detection set. The second is a whole-frame reel
screening set. We then run a large controlled search over the architectural
design space. The search covers 78 YOLOv8-family variants and 786 five-fold
training runs. From it we draw practical rules about when complexity helps.
Guided by these rules, we build an efficient detector and validate it on both
datasets with accuracy, speed, statistical tests, and interpretability.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We study online gambling-promotion detection on two complementary public
datasets that together cover fine-grained logo detection and whole-frame
screening.
\item We run a large, controlled architectural design-space search (786 runs,
78 variants, five-fold cross-validation) and report two practitioner-facing
findings: most complexity hurts on small data, and architecture ranking does not
transfer between datasets.
\item Guided by the search, we propose an efficient real-time detector built from
space-to-depth downsampling, ShuffleNet blocks, and parameter-free SimAM
attention. It reaches the best accuracy on the hard dataset among all standard
and modified nano detectors we tested, at $115$ FPS.
\item We report accuracy jointly with parameters, FLOPs, and measured latency,
add statistical tests, and use Grad-CAM to explain model behaviour. We release a
fully reproducible protocol.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Single-stage object detection}
Two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN generate region proposals and then
classify them~\cite{ren2015faster}. They are accurate but slow. Single-stage
detectors predict boxes directly and are faster. YOLO framed detection as one
regression problem~\cite{redmon2016you}. SSD added multi-scale feature
maps~\cite{liu2016ssd}, and RetinaNet introduced focal loss for foreground
imbalance~\cite{lin2017focal}. Successive YOLO revisions refined the backbone,
neck, and training recipe~\cite{redmon2018yolov3, bochkovskiy2020yolov4}. The
Ultralytics YOLOv8 family~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} is a widely used, deployable
baseline, and forms the basis of this work. EfficientDet made the
accuracy--efficiency trade-off explicit through compound
scaling~\cite{tan2020efficientdet}; we adopt that trade-off view at the level of
individual building blocks.

\subsection{Attention and multi-scale modules}
Channel attention reweights feature maps by learned importance.
Squeeze-and-Excitation uses global pooling and a small
gate~\cite{hu2018squeeze}. CBAM adds spatial attention~\cite{woo2018cbam}, and
coordinate attention encodes position into channel
weights~\cite{hou2021coordinate}. ECA-Net reduces the cost of channel attention
with a local convolution~\cite{wang2020eca}. SimAM is a parameter-free attention
module derived from an energy function, adding no
parameters~\cite{yang2021simam}; this property makes it attractive for efficient
detectors. On the multi-scale axis, Res2Net builds hierarchical residual
connections inside a block~\cite{gao2021res2net}, and residual learning
underpins deeper designs~\cite{he2016deep}. Space-to-depth convolution replaces
strided downsampling and preserves information for small
objects~\cite{sunkara2022no}. Transformer backbones such as
ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image} and Swin~\cite{liu2021swin} offer global
context at higher cost. These modules help on large benchmarks, but their value
on small task-specific datasets is not guaranteed.

\subsection{Efficient architectures}
Efficient backbones reduce parameters and computation. MobileNetV2 uses inverted
residuals~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}. ShuffleNetV2 uses grouped convolutions
and channel shuffle for cheap feature mixing~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}. GhostNet
generates features with cheap linear operations~\cite{han2020ghostnet}. These
designs trade some accuracy for deployability. Model complexity interacts
strongly with dataset size: on small task-specific data, a larger model is not
reliably better. This motivates evaluating each block against its cost rather
than in isolation.

\subsection{Visual detection of illicit and gambling content}
Detecting gambling promotion is a visual content-moderation task related to logo
and brand detection in the wild. The target marks are small, varied, and appear
over dynamic backgrounds. Public datasets for this specific task are scarce. We
use two recent community datasets to fill this
gap~\cite{dataset_v21_roboflow, dataset_judi3k_kaggle}. The first isolates
gambling brand logos as objects; the second labels whole reel frames as gambling
or not. Together they cover both fine-grained detection and coarse frame-level
screening, a dual view uncommon in prior logo-detection work.

\subsection{Research gap}
Prior work typically proposes a single modified detector and reports an aggregate
accuracy gain on one dataset. It rarely isolates which building blocks help, how
robust the ranking is across datasets, or whether the added complexity survives a
real latency budget. For gambling-promotion detection specifically, there is no
systematic study of the architectural design space and no deployment-oriented
detector validated on both logo-level and frame-level data. We address this gap
with a large controlled search, honest statistics, and an efficient detector
benchmarked on accuracy and measured speed.

\section{Method}
We first describe the design-space search that motivates our detector. We then
describe the proposed model and its building blocks.

\subsection{Design-space search}
We treat the detector as a base YOLOv8n topology into which we insert candidate
blocks along three axes: downsampling (standard strided convolution versus
space-to-depth), backbone unit (standard C2f versus Ghost or ShuffleNet
variants), and attention (none, SE, ECA, CBAM, coordinate attention, SimAM, or
GAM). Recombining these axes, together with a few transformer and multi-scale
options, yields a family of 78 variants that all share the same neck and
detection head. Every variant is trained under one fixed protocol and one fixed
set of cross-validation folds, so that any difference is attributable to the
architecture. The full search comprises 786 successful training runs.

The search serves two purposes. It maps which blocks help under data scarcity,
and it provides the evidence for the winning recipe used by our detector. We
report the search results in Section~\ref{sec:search}.

\subsection{Proposed detector}
Guided by the search, the proposed detector combines three efficient blocks on
top of the YOLOv8n topology, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.

\emph{Space-to-depth downsampling.} Standard strided convolution discards
spatial detail that small logos depend on. We replace it with a space-to-depth
(SPD) operator~\cite{sunkara2022no}. SPD rearranges a spatial block into the
channel dimension before a non-strided convolution, so no pixel information is
lost during downsampling. For an input of size $H\times W\times C$ and a scale
$s$, SPD produces $\frac{H}{s}\times\frac{W}{s}\times s^2C$ features, which a
subsequent convolution mixes.

\emph{ShuffleNet blocks.} We use ShuffleNet units with channel shuffle and
grouped convolutions~\cite{ma2018shufflenet}. They mix features cheaply and keep
the parameter count low, which supports the efficiency goal.

\emph{Parameter-free SimAM attention.} We add SimAM attention~\cite{yang2021simam}.
SimAM assigns each neuron an importance from an energy function without adding
any parameters. For a feature $x$ in a channel with mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2$ over the spatial positions, the neuron energy is
\begin{equation}
e_t = \frac{4(\sigma^2+\lambda)}{(t-\mu)^2 + 2\sigma^2 + 2\lambda},
\end{equation}
and the refined feature is $\tilde{x} = x \cdot \mathrm{sigmoid}(1/e)$. Because
it adds no parameters and negligible computation, SimAM improves attention at
essentially no cost.

Together these blocks embody the design-space finding: cheap detail-preserving
downsampling and parameter-free attention, rather than heavy capacity, give the
best accuracy per unit of cost on this task.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
We use two complementary Indonesian datasets, with examples in
Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}. The first, \textbf{Judol Detection
v2}~\cite{dataset_v21_roboflow}, is a fine-grained gambling-logo detection set of
443 images across five brand classes (BK8, Gate-of-olympus, Princess,
Starlight-Princess, Zeus), released under CC BY 4.0. Each object is a compact
logo inside a larger scene, so this is the discriminative, hard test bed. The
second, the \textbf{Online Gambling Logo in Instagram Reels Video}
dataset~\cite{dataset_judi3k_kaggle} (hereafter Instagram Reels), contains 3000
whole video frames labelled gambling or nongambling. Because it labels whole
frames rather than cropped objects, it is a frame-level screening task on which
strong models saturate; we use it as a performance-preservation check.

\subsection{Preprocessing and augmentation}
All images are letterboxed to $640\times640$ and normalised. Training
augmentation uses mosaic, random translation of $0.1$, scaling of $0.5$, and
horizontal flip of $0.5$; mosaic is disabled for the final ten epochs to
stabilise convergence. The same augmentation is used for every model.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and protocol}
We evaluate every model with stratified five-fold cross-validation on each
dataset. Folds are stratified by the dominant class of each image and held fixed
across all models, so each model sees identical splits. Cross-validation gives a
more reliable estimate than a single split on small
data~\cite{kohavi1995study}. Accuracy is measured by mean average precision at
IoU $0.5$ (mAP50) and averaged over IoU $0.5$--$0.95$ (mAP50-95); we also report
precision and recall. Model cost is reported as parameter count and GFLOPs for a
single $640\times640$ forward pass, and as measured inference latency. Latency
and frames-per-second are measured on a single NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU and on CPU,
averaged over 60 forward passes after ten warm-up passes. Training uses SGD with
initial learning rate $0.01$, momentum $0.937$, weight decay $0.0005$, batch size
$16$, 100 epochs with early stopping at patience $30$, and seed $0$; the software
stack is PyTorch 2.12 with CUDA 13.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Because the discriminative dataset is small and uses five folds, we test whether
the observed differences are reliable rather than assume them. We compare the
proposed model against the baseline with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on
per-fold mAP50-95, and compare the group of leading models with the Friedman
test~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. We report the resulting $p$-values in full and
draw conclusions accordingly, making no significance claim that the data do not
support.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The architectural design space}
\label{sec:search}
Fig.~\ref{fig:designspace} plots every variant by its accuracy on the two
datasets. Three patterns stand out. First, most added complexity hurts on the
hard dataset: only 13 of 79 variants (16.5\%) beat the plain baseline, and the
bulk cluster below it. Second, a few heavy fusion models collapse
catastrophically on the easy dataset, dropping from the $0.99$ ceiling to as low
as $0.53$ mAP50-95. Third, the newer standard families are not automatically
better here: YOLO26n, the newest and smallest, scores worst of the standard
detectors on the hard dataset (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).

The two datasets also rank architectures differently. The Spearman rank
correlation between the hard and easy datasets is only $\rho=0.49$ over the 78
models present on both. A model that leads on one dataset is only weakly likely
to lead on the other. This is a cautionary result for practice: selecting an
architecture on a single benchmark is unsafe, and validation on more than one
dataset is necessary.

The variants that do beat the baseline share a recipe. They combine
detail-preserving space-to-depth downsampling, efficient Ghost or ShuffleNet
backbone units, and a lightweight attention module. Heavy multi-scale modules
such as Res2Net, and heavy attention, are consistently among the worst. This
recipe motivates the proposed detector.

\subsection{Comparison with standard and modified detectors}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} compares the proposed detector against four standard
nano detectors and four representative design-space variants. On the hard
dataset the proposed model reaches the best mAP50 ($0.8836$), the best mAP50-95
($0.5937$), and the best recall ($0.833$). It beats every standard detector,
including the newer YOLO11n, YOLO12n, and YOLO26n, which are smaller but degrade
markedly on this small dataset (YOLO26n reaches only $0.5381$ mAP50-95). On the
easy dataset all strong models saturate near $0.995$, and the proposed model
keeps the highest recall, so it does not regress on frame-level screening.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the tests. The proposed model beats the baseline in
mean mAP50-95 and in four of five folds, but the Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives
$p=0.068$, and the Friedman test across the leading models gives $p=0.053$.
Neither reaches significance at $\alpha=0.05$. With only five folds, this is the
expected outcome for the small absolute differences involved, and we do not claim
a statistically proven accuracy advantage. The consistent direction of the
improvement, its stability across folds, and the accompanying speed and
interpretability evidence together support the practical value of the detector,
while the honest reporting of the tests sets a realistic expectation.

\subsection{Per-class analysis}
The residual error is concentrated. Fig.~\ref{fig:cm} shows the confusion matrix
of the proposed model on the hard dataset. Four of the five classes are detected
with high recall. One class, BK8, is far harder, with about half of its instances
confused with background. The BK8 mark is a small, text-like wordmark that blends
into busy sports and advertising scenes. Notably, BK8 is not a rare class, so its
difficulty stems from appearance, not scarcity. This points to the clearest path
to a better detector: higher-resolution or tiled inference aimed at small,
text-like marks.

\section{Discussion}
The results carry three messages for building deployable gambling-content
detectors. First, complexity is not free and is usually not helpful on small
domain data. Across 78 variants, five out of six modifications failed to beat a
plain baseline, and some heavy fusion models failed badly. A practitioner should
therefore treat added blocks with suspicion and measure their cost. Second,
architecture ranking does not transfer across datasets. The weak rank
correlation between the logo-level and frame-level datasets means a model chosen
on one may disappoint on the other. Multi-dataset validation is not optional for
this task. Third, the modifications that do help are the cheap ones. Space-to-depth
downsampling and parameter-free attention improve accuracy at little cost, while
heavy capacity does not. Our detector packages this recipe and reaches the best
accuracy among the tested nano detectors at real-time speed.

The improvement over the baseline is modest and not statistically significant at
five folds, which bounds how strongly we can claim an accuracy advantage. The
contribution is therefore best read as a deployment-oriented package: an
efficient detector that is competitive on accuracy, real-time in speed,
interpretable, and validated on two complementary datasets, together with a
design-space map that tells practitioners which blocks are worth their cost.

Several limitations remain. Both datasets are Indonesian and centre on a limited
set of brands, so external validity to other regions and platforms is untested.
The frame-level dataset saturates, which limits what it can reveal. The
statistical power is bounded by five folds. Latency is measured on one GPU and
one CPU and would differ on edge hardware. These points define the natural next
steps.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied efficient detection of online gambling promotion on social media. A
large controlled design-space search of 786 runs over 78 detector variants showed
that most added complexity hurts on small domain data, that a few heavy models
collapse, and that architecture ranking transfers poorly between a logo-level and
a frame-level dataset. Guided by the search, we proposed an efficient detector
built from space-to-depth downsampling, ShuffleNet blocks, and parameter-free
SimAM attention. It reached the best accuracy on the hard dataset among all
standard and modified nano detectors, including the newer YOLO11n, YOLO12n, and
YOLO26n, while running at 115 FPS. We reported accuracy jointly with cost and
measured speed, tested the differences statistically, and explained the model
with Grad-CAM. Future work should extend the search to more datasets and
regions, target the small text-like logo class with higher-resolution inference,
and measure latency on edge devices for real deployment.

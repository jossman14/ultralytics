\begin{abstract}
The YOLO family releases new versions rapidly. Each is presented as faster
and lighter than the last. Practitioners often assume the newest version is
the best choice. We test this assumption for a small, domain-specific task.
The task is detecting online gambling promotion. We benchmark four standard
nano detectors: YOLOv8n, YOLO11n, YOLO12n, and YOLO26n. We use two Indonesian
datasets. The first is a five-class gambling-logo set of 443 images. The
second is a two-class Instagram-reel set of 3000 frames. All models share
identical five-fold cross-validation, augmentation, and training settings. We
report accuracy, parameters, computation, and measured inference speed. The
result is clear and counterintuitive. On the hard logo dataset, accuracy falls
monotonically from the oldest model to the newest. YOLOv8n reaches 0.586
mAP50-95 while YOLO26n reaches only 0.538. A Friedman test confirms the trend
is statistically significant. The newer models are smaller, yet less accurate
here, and one is also slower. The degradation concentrates on the hardest,
text-like logo class. On the easy screening dataset all models saturate. We
conclude that a newer YOLO version is not automatically better on small
domain-specific data. Version choice should be validated on the target task.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The YOLO family is the default choice for real-time object
detection~\cite{redmon2016you, jocher2023yolov8}. New versions appear
frequently. Each release reports gains on large benchmarks such as
COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoft}. Each is usually smaller and faster than the
last. A common assumption follows. Practitioners often pick the newest version
by default. They assume newer means better.

This assumption is rarely tested on small, domain-specific tasks. Many real
applications have only a few hundred labelled images. On such data, the gains
reported on large benchmarks may not hold. A smaller model can even underfit or
generalise worse. The safest choice is therefore not obvious.

We study this question for a concrete and socially relevant task. Online
gambling promotion spreads across social media through brand logos and short
videos. It often targets young audiences. In many countries, including
Indonesia, the activity is illegal. Automated visual detection can support
content moderation. The available datasets are small, which makes the version
question sharp.

This paper benchmarks four standard nano detectors on this task. We compare
YOLOv8n, YOLO11n, YOLO12n, and YOLO26n. We do not modify the architectures. We
evaluate them under one identical protocol on two datasets. We report accuracy
jointly with parameters, computation, and measured speed. We also test the
differences statistically.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we provide a controlled benchmark of
four YOLO generations for gambling-content detection. Second, we show a clear
and counterintuitive result: on the hard dataset, accuracy falls monotonically
as the version gets newer, and the trend is statistically significant. Third,
we locate the degradation in the hardest logo class and confirm no regression
on the easy task. Fourth, we give a practical recommendation: validate version
choice on the target task, because newer is not automatically better on small
domain data.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Evolution of the YOLO family}
YOLO framed detection as a single regression problem~\cite{redmon2016you}.
Later versions refined the backbone, neck, and training
recipe~\cite{redmon2018yolov3, bochkovskiy2020yolov4}. Ultralytics
YOLOv8~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} became a widely used, deployable baseline.
YOLO11 refined the backbone for a better accuracy-efficiency
balance~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}. YOLO12 introduced an attention-centric
design~\cite{tian2025yolov12}. YOLO26 is an end-to-end, NMS-free nano release
aimed at edge devices~\cite{ultralytics2025yolo26}. Each newer version reduces
parameters while claiming competitive accuracy on COCO. Whether these gains
transfer to small domain-specific data is our question.

\subsection{Attention and efficient design}
Newer detectors lean on attention and efficient blocks. Channel attention
reweights features by learned importance~\cite{hu2018squeeze, wang2020eca}.
Attention-centric backbones bring global context at a runtime
cost~\cite{tian2025yolov12}. Efficient units such as
ShuffleNetV2~\cite{ma2018shufflenet} and MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}
cut parameters and computation. These designs are tuned to help in the
large-benchmark regime. On small data, the same designs can trade away accuracy
that the task cannot spare.

\subsection{Detectors on small and domain-specific data}
On large benchmarks, newer and larger designs usually help. On small
task-specific data the picture differs. Cross-validation gives a more reliable
estimate than a single split on small data~\cite{kohavi1995study}. Two-stage
and single-stage detectors alike~\cite{ren2015faster, liu2016ssd, lin2017focal,
tan2020efficientdet} were tuned on large corpora. Their behaviour on a few
hundred images is not guaranteed. This motivates a controlled benchmark rather
than an assumption.

\subsection{Detecting online gambling promotion}
Detecting gambling promotion is a visual content-moderation task. The target
marks are small and appear over dynamic backgrounds. Public datasets are
scarce. We use two recent community datasets~\cite{dataset_v21_roboflow,
dataset_judi3k_kaggle}. One isolates gambling brand logos as objects. The other
labels whole reel frames. Together they cover fine-grained detection and
frame-level screening.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
We use two Indonesian datasets, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}. The first,
\textbf{Judol Detection v2}~\cite{dataset_v21_roboflow}, holds 443 images across
five gambling-brand classes. The classes are BK8, Gate-of-olympus, Princess,
Starlight-Princess, and Zeus. It is released under CC BY 4.0. Its objects are
compact logos in larger scenes. This makes it the discriminative, hard test
bed. The second, the \textbf{Online Gambling Logo in Instagram Reels Video}
dataset~\cite{dataset_judi3k_kaggle} (Instagram Reels), holds 3000 whole frames
labelled gambling or nongambling. It labels whole frames, not cropped objects.
It is a frame-level screening task on which strong models saturate.

Table~\ref{tab:classdist} lists the class distribution of the hard dataset. The
five classes are moderately balanced. BK8 is the second most common class. Yet
BK8 is the hardest to detect, as shown later. Its difficulty comes from small,
text-like logos, not from scarcity.

\subsection{Benchmarked models}
We benchmark four standard nano detectors. \textbf{YOLOv8n} (2023) is the widely
used baseline~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}. \textbf{YOLO11n} (2024) refines the
backbone for efficiency~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}. \textbf{YOLO12n} (2025) adopts
an attention-centric design~\cite{tian2025yolov12}. \textbf{YOLO26n} (2025) is
an end-to-end, NMS-free release for edge
devices~\cite{ultralytics2025yolo26}. We use the official configurations without
modification. Each newer model is smaller than the last. This lets us test
whether the size reduction costs accuracy on our task.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
We report accuracy with mean average precision. Precision and recall come from
the detection counts. Let $\mathrm{TP}$, $\mathrm{FP}$, and $\mathrm{FN}$ denote
true positives, false positives, and false negatives:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},
\qquad
\mathrm{Recall} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}} .
\end{equation}
Average precision (AP) is the area under the precision--recall curve. Mean
average precision averages AP over all $K$ classes:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP} = \int_0^1 p(r)\,dr,
\qquad
\mathrm{mAP} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{AP}_k .
\end{equation}
We report mAP50 at IoU $0.5$ and mAP50-95 averaged over IoU $0.5$ to $0.95$. We
also report parameters, GFLOPs for a $640\times640$ forward pass, and measured
inference speed.

\subsection{Fold construction}
We build five folds with stratified sampling. Cross-validation is more reliable
than a single split on small data~\cite{kohavi1995study}. The strata are the
dominant class of each image. Stratification keeps class ratios stable across
folds. The split uses a fixed seed of 0. The same folds are reused for every
model. This removes split luck as a confounder.

\subsection{Training and evaluation protocol}
Training settings are identical across all four models. Images are letterboxed
to $640\times640$. Augmentation uses mosaic, translation $0.1$, scaling $0.5$,
and horizontal flip $0.5$. Mosaic is disabled for the final ten epochs. Training
uses SGD with initial learning rate $0.01$, momentum $0.937$, and weight decay
$0.0005$. The batch size is $16$ and the seed is $0$. Each model trains for 100
epochs with early stopping at patience $30$. Inference speed is measured on a
single NVIDIA RTX A4000 over 60 forward passes after ten warm-up passes. The
stack is PyTorch 2.12 with CUDA 13.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Main comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the full results. On the hard dataset, YOLOv8n
obtains the best mAP50 and mAP50-95. The newer models trail on every accuracy
metric. YOLO26n, the newest and smallest, is the weakest. It reaches only
$0.5381$ mAP50-95 against the YOLOv8n $0.5857$.

The trend is monotonic with release order. Each newer version scores below the
one before it. This is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:trend}. The gap between the
oldest and newest model is $0.048$ mAP50-95. That gap is large for a
five-class detection task of this size.

The complexity columns tell the other half. Each newer model uses fewer
parameters and fewer GFLOPs. The saving does not buy accuracy here. It costs
accuracy instead. The next subsections examine speed, stability, and where the
loss occurs.

\subsection{Performance preservation on Instagram Reels}
The easy dataset is a preservation check. All four models score near $0.99$
mAP50-95 on it (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The frame-level task carries strong
holistic cues. It does not separate the versions.

The small differences that remain do not follow the hard-dataset order. YOLO12n
even ties for the best value here. This confirms that the version differences
surface only on the harder task.

A single easy benchmark would therefore hide the effect. This is itself a
lesson: an easy dataset can make all versions look equal. Evaluation must
include a discriminative task.

\subsection{Accuracy versus complexity}
Table~\ref{tab:relative} makes the trade explicit. Each newer model is smaller
but less accurate. YOLO26n saves $18.5\%$ of the parameters. It loses $0.048$
mAP50-95 in return. YOLO12n and YOLO11n follow the same pattern with smaller
losses. The parameter saving is real but unhelpful on this data.

On small domain-specific data, the newer designs trade away accuracy that the
task cannot spare. The efficient blocks that help on large benchmarks do not
pay off here. Capacity is not the bottleneck; data is.

\subsection{Inference speed}
Fewer parameters do not guarantee faster inference.
Fig.~\ref{fig:speed} shows measured GPU throughput. YOLOv8n is the fastest at
$137$ FPS. All four run above the $30$ FPS real-time threshold.

YOLO12n is the slowest at $58$ FPS despite having fewer parameters. Its
attention-centric design adds runtime cost~\cite{tian2025yolov12}. The
parameter count hides this cost. Speed must be measured, not inferred from size.

For a deployment that needs both accuracy and speed, the oldest model is the
strongest here. It is both the most accurate and the fastest. The newer models
offer neither advantage on this task.

\subsection{Per-fold stability}
Each model varies by about $0.07$ mAP50-95 across folds, shown by the error
bars in Fig.~\ref{fig:trend}. The fold variation does not overturn the ranking.
YOLOv8n stays highest in most folds and YOLO26n stays lowest. The order is
stable, not an artifact of one split. This consistency is why the statistical
test later confirms the effect.

\subsection{Precision and recall}
Table~\ref{tab:pr} reports precision and recall. YOLOv8n keeps a strong balance
of both. The newest YOLO26n has the lowest recall by a wide margin at $0.700$.

The recall drop is the main failure of the newer models. They miss more
gambling logos. In a moderation setting, a missed logo is content that slips
through. Recall matters most for this task.

The precision differences are smaller. The newer models do not trade recall for
precision; they simply detect less. This points to reduced capacity on small
objects, which the per-class analysis confirms.

\subsection{Training convergence}
Fig.~\ref{fig:conv} plots the fold-mean mAP50-95 against the epoch. All four
curves rise quickly and then plateau. The curves separate by version late in
training.

YOLOv8n converges to the highest plateau. YOLO26n settles lowest. The ordering
appears early and holds to the end. The gap is not a checkpoint artifact.

The convergence view rules out under-training as the cause. Every model trains
for the same schedule with early stopping. The newer models simply reach a lower
ceiling on this data.

\subsection{Where the loss occurs: confusion matrices}
Fig.~\ref{fig:cm} compares the confusion matrices of the best and worst models.
YOLOv8n keeps strong diagonal values for four of five classes. YOLO26n weakens

The main error is confusion with background. Both models miss some BK8 logos.

The newer model does not confuse one logo for another. It simply fails to detect

\subsection{Per-class detection difficulty}
Table~\ref{tab:perclass} breaks accuracy down by class. Four of the five classes
are relatively easy. All models detect them above $0.86$ mAP50.

BK8 is the hardest class for every model. It is a small, text-like wordmark on
busy scenes. The newest model loses the most on it. BK8 mAP50 falls from $0.612$
for YOLOv8n to $0.466$ for YOLO26n.

The version degradation concentrates on this one hard class, as
Table~\ref{tab:perclass} and Fig.~\ref{fig:perclass} show. The easy classes
barely change. This locates the weakness precisely: the newer, smaller models
struggle most with small, text-like marks. Higher-resolution or tiled inference
is the clearest remedy.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the tests. The Friedman test across the four
models gives $p=0.011$. The differences are significant at $\alpha=0.05$.

The pairwise Wilcoxon tests against YOLOv8n are consistent in direction. With
five folds they do not each reach significance. The overall Friedman result
still confirms the effect.

The statistics support the visual and per-class evidence. The accuracy
differences among the four generations are real, not noise. The monotonic trend
is a genuine effect on this task.

\subsection{Discussion}
The results caution against a common habit. Adopting the newest YOLO version by
default is not safe on small domain-specific data. The newer designs optimise
for the large-benchmark regime. There, efficient backbones and attention pay
off. On a few hundred images of small logos, the same designs lose accuracy, and
one loses speed as well.

We now examine why the degradation occurs. The newer models are smaller by
design. Each trims parameters to improve efficiency on COCO. On a large dataset,
the trimmed capacity is still enough to fit the data. On 443 images the picture
is different. The task does not cause overfitting that extra capacity would
waste. Instead the larger YOLOv8n capacity acts as useful headroom. The smaller
models reach a lower ceiling because they have less of it. Fewer parameters here
mean a lower accuracy limit, not a free saving.

The attention-centric YOLO12n illustrates a second cause. Attention layers learn
where to look from data. They need many examples to learn useful attention
patterns. A few hundred images do not provide that signal. The attention then
adds cost without a matching benefit. This shows in two ways. YOLO12n is less
accurate than YOLOv8n, and it is also the slowest at $58$ FPS. Its runtime cost
is real while its data-hungry benefit does not appear.

The end-to-end YOLO26n shows a third cause. It removes non-maximum suppression
and learns a one-to-one label assignment. This design needs abundant data to
learn stable assignments. On a small dataset the assignment is harder to learn.
The effect appears as the sharp recall drop to $0.700$ in Table~\ref{tab:pr}.
The model misses more logos rather than misclassifying them. This is the most
costly error for moderation, where a missed promo slips through.

The degradation is therefore regime-specific, not a flaw in the newer models.
On large datasets their efficient designs would likely pay off as intended. The
lesson is about matching a model to a data regime. Small, fine-grained tasks
favour capacity and mature, well-understood designs. This is also why the loss
concentrates on the small BK8 class: fine detail is exactly what a trimmed model
sacrifices first.

The practical recommendation is simple. Version choice should be validated on
the target task and dataset. It should not be assumed from release order. The
oldest model in our study, YOLOv8n, is the best all-round choice here. It is the
most accurate and the fastest.

Reporting accuracy jointly with measured speed guards against a misleading
conclusion. Testing the differences statistically guards against reading noise
as signal. Using both an easy and a hard dataset guards against a saturated
benchmark hiding the effect. These practices matter for any small-data
deployment.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
We group the limitations by the kind of validity they affect.

\textit{External validity.} Both datasets are Indonesian and centre on a limited
set of brands. Results may not transfer to other regions or platforms. More
datasets would strengthen the claim.

\textit{Statistical conclusion validity.} The hard dataset is small and uses
five folds. The Friedman test is significant, but the pairwise tests are
underpowered. More folds or datasets would tighten the pairwise evidence.

\textit{Construct validity.} We measure cost as parameters, GFLOPs, and GPU
latency. Latency on edge hardware may differ. Edge timing is a natural next
step.

\section{Conclusion}
We benchmarked four YOLO generations, YOLOv8n through YOLO26n, for online
gambling-content detection on small domain-specific data. On the hard logo
dataset, accuracy fell monotonically as the version got newer, from $0.586$ to
$0.538$ mAP50-95, and a Friedman test confirmed the trend is significant. The
newer models were smaller but less accurate, and the attention-centric YOLO12n
was also the slowest. The degradation concentrated on the hardest, text-like
BK8 class. On the easy screening dataset all models saturated. The lesson is
that a newer YOLO version is not automatically better on small domain-specific
data, and version choice should be validated on the target task. Future work
will extend the benchmark to more datasets and regions, target the small
text-like logo class with higher-resolution inference, and measure latency on
edge devices.
